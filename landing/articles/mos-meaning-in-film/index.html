On-set terminology

What does MOS mean in film?

MOS on a slate or in a script means the shot was recorded picture-only, no synchronized sound. The story you'll read everywhere, that it stands for a German director's broken-English "mit out sound", is almost certainly false. It just makes a better anecdote than the actual answer, so it's the one that survived.

The "mit out sound" story doesn't hold up

Nearly every top-ranking article on MOS repeats the same anecdote as settled fact: a German-émigré director, often named as Erich von Stroheim, told his crew "ve'll shoot dis mit out sound", and the crew wrote MOS on the slate as a joke that stuck. It's a great story. It also breaks on contact with the language it claims to come from: the German word for "without" is ohne, not "mit out". A native German speaker saying "with out" instead of "without" is the kind of mistake that fits an American ear's idea of a German accent, not an actual German sentence. Wikipedia's own entry on the term calls the story "presumed to be mythical" for exactly this reason, and notes a second version of the anecdote where the director says "mit ohne sprechen", "with without speaking", which is closer to real German but a different joke entirely.

None of that stops the story from being repeated as unqualified fact by most of the film-education sites that rank for this term. It's a fun line, and nobody fact-checks a slate abbreviation. But if you're citing an origin, it's worth knowing the popular one is the least likely one.

The more likely origin: motor only

The technical explanation with the strongest footing is "motor only sync" or "motor only shot". Early sound-era cameras needed to run in sync with a separate sound recorder, originally via a physical cable, later via a matched crystal-driven motor, so that picture and audio could be lined back up in the edit. A camera running on its own motor with nothing to sync to, because no audio was being recorded for that setup, was logged as running "motor only". MOS as a production-report abbreviation traces more plausibly to that phrase than to a joke, because it's the kind of dry, technical shorthand slates and reports are actually full of.

Other theories exist and nobody claims certainty: "minus optical sound", "minus optical stripe", and "music on side" all show up in older glossaries. The honest answer to "what does MOS stand for" is that there is no single sourced origin everyone agrees on, motor-only-sync is the best-supported guess, and mit-out-sound is the one that spread because it's funnier.

MOS is not the same as wild sound

These two get confused because they're both "sound that isn't sync sound", but they're opposites in practice:

How it actually gets marked, and where sets disagree

The textbook version says the 2nd AC holds the clapper sticks together and calls "MOS" instead of clapping them shut, because there's no sync point to mark. In practice, sets are split on this, and it's worth knowing before you assume one way is wrong:

  • Sticks held openFingers through the hinge, sticks visibly apart on camera. The logic: closing them implies a sync point that doesn't exist and could mislead someone reviewing footage later.
  • Sticks clapped anywaySome crews still clap MOS takes shut out of habit and for a clean visual frame reference on playback, with MOS written large on the board so it's unmistakable either way.

Both are common. What actually matters is that MOS is legible on the board itself, written large, not just implied by the sticks' position, because a slate photographed briefly or out of focus is the only record some crew members will see of that call.

When productions shoot MOS on purpose

Cutting the sound department out of a setup isn't a compromise, it's often a deliberate choice: b-roll and cutaway inserts, plates for VFX, pickups that will be fully ADR'd anyway, second-unit work where carrying a boom operator and mixer isn't worth the time, and scenes the director always intended to score wall-to-wall with no production dialogue. Christopher Nolan shot key sequences in Oppenheimer MOS, including moments around the Trinity test, letting the image carry the scene with sound built entirely in post rather than captured live.

Shooting MOS reduces the sound department's workload for that take, not the log's. Scene, setup, and take still need to be recorded exactly as they would with sync sound, see our guide to logging takes on set, and if the take is also a keeper, it still gets marked the same way any other good take does.
