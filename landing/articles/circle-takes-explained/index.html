On-set terminology

What is a circle take?

A circle take is a take the director wants processed and cut in. Most explainers stop there and call it "the mark of a great performance." That's the effect, not the reason. The reason is money: on film, printing every take cost real cash by the foot, so someone had to tell the lab which ones to bother with.

Where the term comes from

On a 35mm shoot, the camera negative from every take gets developed regardless, but developing isn't the expensive part, printing a workprint (or, later, telecine-transferring to video for dailies) is. Processing and printing every single take of every setup was slow and costly enough that productions didn't do it by default. Instead, the director told the script supervisor which takes to circle on the camera report, and the lab printed only the circled ones. "Circle takes" and "print takes" were, for most of film history, the same instruction given twice: once on paper for the record, once as a line item to a lab that billed by the foot.

That's a narrower, more useful answer than "it's the mark of a great performance." It also explains something the vague version can't: why the take doesn't have to be flawless to get circled. A director will circle a take with a technical flaw, a boom shadow, a slightly late cue, because the performance is what they want in the cut and the fix is cheaper in post than another take. Circling was never a purity test. It was an instruction: spend the money on this one.

Circle vs. print: same instruction, two places it lived

On most sets these are used interchangeably today, but they weren't identical documents. The script supervisor circled the take number on her copy of the script or her daily log, for continuity and for the record. The camera report, filled out by the camera assistant and sent to the lab with the film, had its own Print column, checked for the same takes. Two different papers, same list, because two different people downstream needed to see it: the editor reading the script supervisor's notes, and the lab reading the camera report.

What changed once everything got recorded

Digital acquisition removed the cost that made circling necessary in the first place. Every take gets recorded to a card regardless of whether the director likes it, so there's no lab bill to control. The vocabulary survived anyway, because the underlying job didn't go away: someone still has to tell post which takes matter, out of the pile that got shot. "Circle it" on a digital set means the same thing it always meant, mark this as a keeper, it's just no longer an instruction to a lab. It's a flag in a script supervisor's log, a checkbox in a slate app, or a GOOD/GOLD tag on a clip's metadata.

A few places the practice genuinely diverges by workflow, which most single-paragraph definitions flatten out:

  • MulticamOne circle call from the director can apply to a take shared across two or three cameras at once, so the mark has to propagate to every camera's log, not just one.
  • DocumentaryThere's rarely a repeated take structure to circle in the moment. Selects get marked in review afterward, not called out loud on set.
  • Multiple circlesA director sometimes circles two or three takes as live options rather than one winner, leaving the choice to the edit. A circle is a shortlist, not always a verdict.

What a circle take isn't

A circle doesn't guarantee a take makes the final cut, it guarantees it gets a first look. Editors routinely go back into the uncircled takes when the circled one doesn't cut well against its neighbors, especially for reaction shots or timing on a line. The circle tells post where to start looking, not where to stop.

It also isn't the same thing as "good take" in the loose sense people use online. On set, a take can be technically clean and never get circled because the performance wasn't what the director wanted, and a take with a visible flaw can get circled anyway because the performance was.
