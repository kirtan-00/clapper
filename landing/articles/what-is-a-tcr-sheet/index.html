What is a TCR sheet?

Short answer: it depends who you ask, and that's worth saying plainly instead of pretending there's one industry-standard form. Here's what the phrase actually points to, and where the real document already lives on your set.

5 min read

Quick answer

A TCR sheet is a take-by-take log that pairs each take, or each moment inside a take, with its timecode, so anyone in post can jump straight to a specific frame without scrubbing through footage to find it.

It is not a single standardized industry form the way a call sheet or a continuity sheet is. There's no one PDF everyone in the industry agrees is "the" TCR sheet. It's closer to a description of a job a document does than the name of a specific document.

Where the term actually comes from

"TCR" doesn't have one settled expansion. In telecine and broadcast delivery, you'll see it used for timecode reference or timecode reading, mostly in the context of checking a finished program against a reference timecode before it airs, or syncing a transfer back to the original camera negative. That's a post and delivery term, not something that started on a film set.

On the set side, Clapper uses "TCR sheet" as shorthand for a take-by-take record of what happened during each take, timecoded, which is closer to a continuity log with timecodes attached than to the broadcast-QC meaning. Both usages are real. Neither is the one true definition, and if you go looking for a universal "TCR sheet template," you won't find one, because the paperwork under that specific name was never standardized the way, say, a sound report was.

Where the timecode already lives, without anyone calling it a TCR sheet

The useful part isn't the label, it's that most sets already generate this data under other names:

DocumentWhat carries the timecode
Sound reportA timecode column against every take, filled in by the sound mixer as a matter of course.
Camera report / camera log sheetTimecode in and out per clip, critical once more than one camera is rolling and dailies need to sync.
Script supervisor's continuity logReferences timecode once dailies are synced, tying a written note to an exact frame.
DIT verification passChecks offloaded cards for factory-default timecode, a red flag that a camera's internal clock wasn't jam-synced correctly.

Put those four together and you already have everything a "TCR sheet" would claim to be. The gap isn't the data, it's that it's scattered across documents owned by different departments, written at different points in the day, and rarely cross-referenced until someone in the edit needs a specific frame fast.

Why it's worth logging as you shoot, not after

The moment this matters is an editor or a director scrubbing through dailies at the end of a long day, trying to find the one line reading everyone remembered as great. Without a timecoded note pointing at it, that's minutes of scrubbing per take, across every take shot that day. With one, it's a lookup: take 3C, circled, timecode noted at the moment it happened. The value isn't the document format, it's that the timecode got captured live, next to a note about what was actually happening at that frame, instead of reconstructed later from memory.

Where practice differs

On a scripted union US set, both the sound report and the camera report carry a timecode column as a matter of routine, whether or not anyone calls the resulting document a "TCR sheet." On a lot of indie and lower-budget Indian sets, that discipline is inconsistent: timecode gets burned into the picture and the editor is left to eyeball it, because there's no dedicated crew member logging it live. Neither is wrong, they're different amounts of infrastructure for the same underlying problem.

What a usable timecode log actually needs

  • Scene, setup, and take identifier, matching the slate.
  • Timecode in, and camera letter if more than one camera is rolling.
  • A one-line note on what happened at that moment: a great line reading, a boom dip, a prop drop.
  • The take's result: circled, NG, or PU, same as a continuity log.

That's the whole document. It doesn't need a separate name to be useful; it needs to exist, and to exist while the take is still fresh, logged next to the note about what happened rather than reconstructed later from a camera card.
