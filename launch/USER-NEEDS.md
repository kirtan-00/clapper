# Clapper usage research: what real volumes look like, film side and podcast side

Purpose: set tier caps on evidence, not guesswork. Every number below is either a cited figure, a figure I built from two or more cited figures (shown with the math), or a labeled forum anecdote. Where nothing exists, it says so.

Research date: August 2026.

---

## FILM SIDE

### 1. How many productions does a working script supervisor / 1st AD do per month or year

No survey publishes this number. What exists is qualitative, and it says the honest answer is "it depends on the year and the person," not a stable rate.

- Freelance film crew work project to project, and the pattern reported is bursty: "booking a job for three weeks and then nothing for a month, then three commercial jobs all at once." (industry description via [Assemble, Day Rates For Film Crew](https://www.onassemble.com/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-day-rates-for-film-crew-2021))
- Script supervisor day rates run $325-$450/day at IATSE minimums for a 10-hour day, with student/ultra-low-budget work at $0-200/day and commercials paying more per day than indie features because commercial shoots are short (1-5 days) ([Assemble](https://www.onassemble.com/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-day-rates-for-film-crew-2021); rate detail also in [Chron](https://work.chron.com/script-supervisors-paid-28207.html) and [ZipRecruiter](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Salaries/Script-Supervisor-Salary)).
- 1st AD day rate: roughly $550-$650/day for a 10-hour day; no source gives a reliable shoot-days-per-month figure ([onassemble.com](https://www.onassemble.com/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-day-rates-for-film-crew-2021); [needacrew.com](https://www.needacrew.com/blog/film-crew-day-rates-2026)).

Reasoning bridge (not a citation, built from the shoot-length data in question 2 below): a script supervisor or 1st AD who works the commercial/music-video circuit and stays steadily booked could plausibly stack 2-4 short (1-3 day) productions in a single month. Someone who mostly works features works on ONE production for weeks or months at a time, so their monthly *new-project* count is far lower (often under 1/month) even though their day-to-day workload is heavy. This is the single most important structural fact for a cap: **project count and workload are not the same axis.** A feature crew member logs a huge number of takes inside ONE project; a commercial-circuit freelancer creates MANY projects, each small. A cap on projects/month protects against the second pattern, not the first.

### 2. Shoot days by project type

This has real, if scattered, numbers:

- **Short film** (8-10 minute indie short): typically **3 shooting days** ([Quora aggregation](https://www.quora.com/How-many-days-long-is-a-short-film-production-schedule), cross-referenced against general indie-short practice).
- **TV commercial**: shoot itself is usually **1-3 days**, running 8-12 hours/day; the 6-12 week total timeline is mostly pre/post, not shoot days ([Beverly Boy Productions](https://beverlyboy.com/tv-commercial-production/typical-timelines-for-a-tv-commercial-production-schedule/)).
- **Music video**: mid-tier productions run **1-2 shoot days**; the 6-10 week end-to-end timeline is dominated by pre-production (2-4 weeks) and post (2-6 weeks) ([Medium, Garrett Wesley Gibbons](https://medium.com/@garrettgibbons/what-should-a-music-video-budget-be-120bf49f554f); budget-tier detail also in [pixo.video](https://pixo.video/blog/how-much-does-a-music-video-cost)).
- **Indie feature**: larger indie features run **20-30 days**; true low-budget DIY features are shot in as few as **9-15 days**, with extreme micro-budget cases at 2-8 days ([Noam Kroll](https://noamkroll.com/how-to-shoot-a-feature-film-at-lightning-pace-2-day-4-day-8-day-feature-productions-explained/)).
- **Episodic TV (hour-long drama)**: industry-standard is **8 shooting days per episode**, with more ambitious shows running 9-10; crews are expected to complete 7-8 script pages per shoot day to hit this ([Filmustage / Moviejawn aggregation of industry practice](https://filmustage.com/blog/scheduling-for-tv-vs-film-whats-the-difference/)).
- **Corporate/marketing video**: a typical 5-minute piece shoots in **1 day** (forum-level source, treat as anecdote, not survey: [Scribd shot-list discussion](https://www.scribd.com/doc/71968190/Shot-List)).

Takeaway: a "project" in Clapper spans a huge range, from a single 1-day commercial to a 30-day feature. This matters for the podcast-style hours metering question the owner may eventually ask about film too, but for now the product meters by PROJECT COUNT, not shoot days, so the spread mostly affects how much a single project is "worth" to a heavy user, not the cap math directly.

### 3. Takes per shoot day

No survey source quantifies this. Script supervisor materials describe WHAT gets logged (slate, take number, length, retakes, wild tracks) but not a typical daily count ([StudioBinder script supervisor template guide](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/script-supervisor-forms-template/); [SetHero script supervisor report explainer](https://sethero.com/blog/script-supervisor-report-explained/)). This is a real gap; see the gaps section. It doesn't block the cap decision because Clapper's proposed caps are project-based and hour-based, not take-based, but if a future "takes logged" metric is ever considered for metering, this needs primary research (interviewing working script supervisors directly, since it is not published anywhere found).

### 4. Freelance vs staff, and who buys tools

- The overwhelming majority of this workforce is freelance/project-based, not staff, confirmed structurally by the day-rate-per-project pay model across every crew role researched.
- On physical kit: freelancers who bring their own gear (camera, audio, grip) charge the production an extra "kit rate" or "gear rate" on top of their day rate. Production doesn't buy the gear; it rents it from the freelancer for that job ([Video Collective / Freelance Video Collective](https://www.freelancevideocollective.com/filmmaker-resources/production-crew-rates/); [Medium, Jeremiah Warren](https://medium.com/@jeremiahjw/know-your-worth-what-your-peers-charge-for-commercial-productions-f28d9ed739d3)).
- No source addresses SOFTWARE subscriptions specifically (nothing on who pays for a script supervisor's continuity app, editing software seat, etc.). Reasoning by analogy to the kit-rate pattern: software tools that live on a freelancer's personal phone/laptop and follow them between productions (which describes Clapper exactly) are very likely to be bought and owned by the individual, the same way they own their own laptop or slate app, not expensed per-production the way a rented camera package is. This is inference, not a citation, but it is a load-bearing one: **it means the buyer is the freelancer, and freelancer income is variable and often thin between gigs** (see day-rate section above: student/ultra-low-budget work pays $0-200/day, and gaps between bookings are common). That argues for keeping the free tier genuinely usable by a real working freelancer between paid jobs, not just by hobbyists, and for pricing the paid tier at a level a freelancer clears easily once they're busy enough to need it (i.e., price it against the day rate of ONE commercial gig, not against enterprise software norms).

### 5. India specifically

This is the most consequential finding in the film section, because it overturns a Hollywood-crew-structure assumption.

- **The script supervisor role is NOT standard in Indian film production.** Direct quote from a long-form investigation: "most filmmakers choose not to use one," and "Traditionally, Hindi film directors haven't used a script supervisor." The piece found only two people in the entire industry who specialize in the role, out of a market that produces almost 1,000 movies a year ([The Caravan, "The unappreciated work of Bollywood's script supervisors"](https://caravanmagazine.in/reportage/written-off-bollywood-script-supervisors)). Continuity has historically been handled informally or not at all, especially pre-1980s productions, and this has only partially changed.
- One concrete pay-scale data point from that piece: a debut director's office paid five assistant directors for a full month what it paid ONE script supervisor for a single week, indicating the few script supervisors who do work in India are priced as premium specialists, not commodity crew.
- **India's ad-film volume is large and this is where the real per-person project-count pressure would come from, not features.** India produces more than 50,000 TV commercials a year across 3,000+ production houses ([various industry sources synthesized](https://www.statista.com/outlook/amo/advertising/tv-video-advertising/india); volume figure via ad-industry roundups). Simple division (50,000 TVCs / 3,000 houses = ~17/house/year, roughly 1.4/month) is a production-house average, not a per-freelancer number, but it confirms the ad circuit is high-volume and short-cycle exactly like the Western commercial circuit described in section 1.
- **Implication for Clapper's India audience:** the buyer in India is much more likely to be a 1st AD, DOP's assistant, or the DIRECTOR THEMSELVES on ad/music-video sets than a dedicated script supervisor, because that role barely exists there. The product's positioning and free-tier generosity should assume the Indian user is stacking MANY short ad/MV gigs (similar cadence to the Western commercial-circuit heavy user in section 1), not doing prestige feature work with a dedicated continuity person. Regional cinema (Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, etc.) volume specifically was not found in this research; see gaps.

## PODCAST SIDE

### 6. Hours recorded per month, by show type

No source gives a clean "hours recorded per month" figure broken out by weekly-solo vs weekly-interview vs daily. What exists, combined:

- Publishing cadence distribution (Buzzsprout, aggregating 120k+ hosted shows): release-interval buckets of **3-7 days: 33%**, **8-14 days: 40%**, monthly-or-less: 21% combined ([Buzzsprout Podcast Statistics](https://www.buzzsprout.com/blog/podcast-statistics)). A separate source puts weekly release specifically at **46%** and biweekly at **~24%** ([Alitu, "How Often Should I Release New Podcast Episodes in 2026?"](https://alitu.com/creator/content-creation/how-often-should-i-release-new-podcast-episodes/)). These two don't perfectly agree on bucket definitions, but both say weekly-to-biweekly is the dominant cadence among shows that are actively publishing at all.
- Published episode length distribution (Buzzsprout): under 10 min: 15%, 20-40 min: 32%, 40-60 min: 21%, over 60 min: 16% (10-20 min bucket not given, implied ~16%). Combining 20-60 min gives roughly half of all published episodes ([Buzzsprout](https://www.buzzsprout.com/blog/podcast-statistics)).
- Built estimate, weekly show at the median published length (~35-45 min): raw recording time is typically somewhat longer than published length even after accounting for edits (see #8 below for the ratio and its caveats). At a conservative 1.3-1.5x raw-to-published ratio for a reasonably tight solo or two-hander show, that's roughly **45-70 minutes of raw recording per episode**, or **about 3.5-5 hours of recorded audio per month** for a weekly show. This is a derived estimate, not a direct citation, and should be labeled as such anywhere it's used.
- Daily shows (news-format, shorter episodes, e.g. 15-20 min published): using the same ratio logic, roughly **10-15 hours of raw recording per month**.
- No source specifically segments "hobbyist" vs "professional" by recorded hours. This is a real gap (see below).

### 7. The distribution: hobbyist vs professional, and why it matters more than the average

This is the single most important finding for the podcast side, and it's a strong one because it comes from real platform data, not anecdote:

- **90% of all podcasts stop publishing after episode 3** (Podnews, May 2025 report, cited via [Medium/Gilbert Alasa](https://medium.com/@alasagilbert/90-of-podcasts-only-survive-their-first-3-episodes-heres-how-to-keep-yours-strong-sold-4e4466585b52) and corroborated by [WhichPodcast's podfade research](https://whichpodcast.com/research/podfade)).
- **Only 8% of podcasts have ever published 10 or more episodes.**
- **81% of abandoned shows had fewer than 10 episodes total** when they stopped.
- Of the roughly 4 million podcasts that exist, only about 1 in 10 is actively releasing episodes at all in a given period (industry aggregate figure, [thepodcasthost.com industry trends roundup](https://www.thepodcasthost.com/listening/podcast-industry-stats/)).

What this means for cap-setting: the distribution is not a bell curve, it's a cliff. The overwhelming majority of anyone who ever opens a podcast tool is a light or one-shot user by volume, almost by definition, because most podcasts die in their first three episodes. The people who reach sustained weekly or daily cadence (the 8-10% who pass episode 10) are a small, genuinely different population, and they are the ones who would generate real monthly hour volume. A free tier here can be generous in absolute hours and still only be "used up" by that top slice, because the bottom 90% never sustain the cadence needed to hit a moderate monthly cap in the first place.

### 8. Recording session length vs published length

- General estimate across sources: total PRODUCTION time (not raw audio) runs 4-10 hours per finished episode once prep, recording, editing, and publishing are counted ([The Podcast Host](https://www.thepodcasthost.com/planning/podcast-episode-length/); corroborating estimate of 6-10 hrs also appeared in general search synthesis).
- On raw-audio-to-published ratio specifically: one source describes a rough "4:1" work-to-content ratio, but this measures total effort (editing + prep) against finished minutes, not raw recorded minutes against finished minutes, so it should NOT be read as "4x the tape." ([The Audacity to Podcast](https://theaudacitytopodcast.com/how-much-time-does-it-take-to-podcast-tap188/))
- A more directly relevant anecdote, labeled as such: one production guide warns hosts against recording "an hour and a half worth of tape for a 20-minute episode," i.e. a ~4.5:1 raw-to-published ratio, but frames this explicitly as a discipline failure to avoid, not a norm ([Rachel Corbett, podcast production guide](https://rachelcorbett.com.au/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-create-a-podcast-episode/)). A well-run scripted or solo show can run close to **1:1 to 1.5:1** (published to raw), while an unedited multi-guest interview show can run **2:1 to 4:1**. There is no survey-level average; treat any single ratio as a range, not a constant.

## 9. Where the caps should sit

### Film: projects per tier

Reasoning: a "project" costs the user almost nothing to create (it's a container the app makes), so the meaningful signal is how many NEW productions a real person starts in a month, not how big each one is. From section 1-2: the busiest realistic pattern is a commercial/music-video-circuit freelancer stacking multiple 1-3 day gigs, plausibly **2-4 new productions/month** at the high end when fully booked. The typical working freelancer, per the "three weeks on, one month off" pattern, is closer to **0.5-1 new production/month** averaged across a year, with big variance by season. A feature or episodic crew member might start only **1-2 productions a YEAR** even though their day-to-day usage inside that one project is heavy.

- **Free tier: 2 active/new projects per month.** This comfortably covers the typical freelancer (0.5-1/month), the feature/episodic person (who barely creates new projects at all), the indie/student filmmaker, and even lets a light commercial freelancer through most months. It will start to pinch only the person who is genuinely stacking 3+ short gigs in the same month, which per section 1 is a real but smaller, busier-than-typical population.
- **Paid tier: 15 projects per month.** This is set well above the highest realistic sustained rate found (2-4/month for a fully-booked commercial-circuit freelancer), so nobody who is actually working at that pace ever feels capped, while still being a real, finite number rather than "unlimited." Confidence: medium. The 2-4/month ceiling for the heaviest identified persona is a reasoned estimate built from cited shoot-length and booking-pattern data, not a directly surveyed number, so 15 has deliberate headroom built in rather than being tightly fit to evidence that doesn't exist yet.
- **India adjustment:** given the ad-circuit volume (50,000 TVCs/year, section 5) and the ABSENCE of a dedicated script-supervisor role, the realistic Indian buyer (1st AD, director, DOP assistant) is plausibly at the higher end of the commercial-circuit pattern, not the feature/episodic end. The free-tier cap of 2/month may bind sooner for this segment than for a Western feature-leaning user; this is worth watching in usage data after launch rather than assuming from research alone.

### Podcast: hours per tier

Reasoning: from sections 6-8, a weekly show runs roughly 3.5-5 recorded hours/month, a daily show 10-15 hours/month, and per section 7, over 90% of anyone who ever tries podcasting never sustains a cadence that would generate serious monthly volume at all.

- **Free tier: 3 hours of recording per month.** This covers a monthly-or-slower hobbyist entirely, and covers most of a weekly show's typical month too (3.5-5 hrs estimated), so it will occasionally pinch a disciplined weekly host but not a hobbyist. Given that 90% of podcasts don't survive past episode 3 (section 7), most people evaluating the free tier will never come close to using it up.
- **Paid tier: 20 hours of recording per month.** This clears even the daily-show estimate (10-15 hrs/month) with real headroom, and covers a host running two concurrent weekly shows or occasional long-form (2+ hour) interview sessions. Confidence: low-to-medium, because the underlying per-episode raw-hours estimates in section 6 are built estimates (published length x an inferred ratio), not directly measured recording-time data. Treat 20 hours as a reasonable ceiling given what's known, not a precisely evidenced number.
- **Top-20%-pay framing:** because the real distribution is a cliff (90% gone by episode 3, only 8% ever reach 10+ episodes), the free/paid line for podcasts is less about splitting a bell curve at the 80th percentile and more about making sure the free tier is generous enough that it never becomes the reason a show dies in its first three episodes, while the paid tier is reserved for the roughly-1-in-10 shows that actually reach sustained cadence. That naturally produces something close to an 80/20 or even 90/10 split without needing to fit the caps tightly to a distribution curve that isn't directly measured.

## 10. The heavy user, as a real person

**Film heavy user:** a freelance 1st AD or script supervisor based in a production hub (Mumbai, LA, or a regional Indian ad-shoot city), working the commercial and music-video circuit rather than features. In a good month she's on three different ad shoots for three different brands, each a 1-3 day shoot with its own client, cast, and crew, back to back with maybe a music video mixed in. She creates a new Clapper project for each one because each has separate footage, a separate edit team, and a separate delivery deadline. In a slow month she might do one job. She owns her phone and pays for her own tools the same way she owns her own slate app or monitor, the production doesn't buy it for her (section 4). She is price-sensitive between gigs and needs the paid tier to be worth it the month she's actually busy, not a fixed cost she carries through her slow months too.

**Podcast heavy user:** someone running two shows out of the same home studio setup, for example a weekly interview show (45-60 min published, recorded a bit long to allow for editing) and a shorter daily or near-daily solo companion show (10-15 min). Between the two, they're recording somewhere in the 10-15+ hour range most months, occasionally more when a long-form guest interview runs 2+ hours raw. They are unambiguously in the roughly-1-in-10 group of podcasters who make it past episode 3 and keep going (section 7), which is exactly the group the paid tier should be built for.

## What could not be found (real gaps, not invented numbers)

- **No survey anywhere gives a direct "productions per month/year" figure for script supervisors or 1st ADs.** Every number in section 1 is inferred from day-rate and shoot-length data, not measured directly. This is the single biggest hole in the film-side research.
- **No source quantifies takes-per-shoot-day.** Script supervisor materials describe the fields logged, not typical daily volume. If Clapper ever meters by takes logged rather than projects, this needs primary research (interviewing working script supervisors), because it does not exist in any published source found.
- **No India-specific volume data for music videos, regional-language cinema (Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Bengali, etc.), or corporate video shoots.** Only the aggregate 50,000 TVC/year figure was found, and it is not broken out by crew role or region.
- **No direct measurement of "hours recorded per month" for podcasters, segmented by show type.** Every figure in section 6 is a built estimate from published episode length plus an inferred raw-to-published ratio, not a directly surveyed number. Nobody appears to publish this.
- **No source segments podcasters into hobbyist vs professional by volume.** The closest available proxy is the episode-count survival data in section 7 (90% gone by episode 3, 8% reach 10+), which is a real, cited distribution, but it measures LONGEVITY, not monthly HOURS. It's a reasonable proxy, not a direct answer.
- **No data found on whether freelance crew expense software subscriptions to productions the way they expense physical kit.** The kit-rate pattern for physical gear is well documented; the software-specific analog in section 4 is reasoned by analogy, explicitly labeled as inference, not evidence.
- **No reliable data on the freelance-vs-staff split by exact percentage** in either film or podcast production. The freelance-dominant pattern is well supported qualitatively (day-rate pay structures across every role researched) but no source gives a clean percentage.
